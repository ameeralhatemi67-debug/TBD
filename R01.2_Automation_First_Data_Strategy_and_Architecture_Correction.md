# R01.2 — Automation-First Data Strategy & Architecture Correction

**Project:** Location-aware discovery platform  
**Document type:** Supporting decision document for R01  
**Status:** Architecture correction / research-direction update  
**Depends on:** `R01_Source_Rights_and_Economics.md`  
**Purpose:** Preserve the strongest findings from R01 while correcting the operating model implied by its verification-cost scenario.

---

# 1. Why R01.2 Exists

R01 successfully answered an important question:

> What kinds of place, event, activity, routing, media, and first-party data can the product legally and economically use?

It produced several strong findings:

- open place-identity sources such as FSQ OS Places and Overture are realistic foundations;
- commercial APIs do not automatically grant rights to build a permanent merged database;
- volatile data such as current hours, event status, price, booking rules, and availability are significantly harder than basic place identity;
- source provenance and field-level rights need to survive normalization;
- reviews, photos, social content, and event inventory often have separate rights and operational constraints;
- event data is harder than static place data;
- a rights-segregated hybrid architecture is safer than indiscriminately ingesting every accessible provider.

R01 also produced a useful but potentially misleading cost scenario.

Its controlled-MVP example assumed a 500-record corpus with routine monthly checks and event/status verification. Under an illustrative labor rate, this yielded approximately:

**SAR 2,333–7,000 per month in human verification labor.**

This estimate was explicitly presented in R01 as an illustrative sensitivity model rather than a mandatory operating cost.

However, the scenario revealed an architectural danger:

> If the product depends on continuously and manually verifying hundreds or thousands of records in every geography, then the project stops behaving like a scalable discovery website and starts behaving like a geographically distributed data-operations company.

R01.2 exists to prevent that assumption from becoming embedded in later research or architecture.

---

# 2. What We Keep From R01

R01 should **not** be discarded.

The following conclusions remain important.

## 2.1 Open / owned identity is strategically valuable

The product should prefer a rights-defensible place-identity foundation wherever possible.

Candidate sources include:

- FSQ OS Places;
- Overture Places;
- Wikidata;
- appropriately licensed government/open data;
- OSM where its ODbL implications are intentionally handled;
- direct operator submissions and agreements.

The product should own its own internal entity identifiers and treat provider identifiers as cross-references.

---

## 2.2 Restricted providers should remain segregated

Commercial or restricted data should not silently flow into the owned canonical corpus.

Examples may include:

- Google Places content;
- paid Foursquare API content;
- Yelp;
- third-party review text;
- ticketing data;
- social-media content;
- provider photos.

These can still be useful in live or licensed workflows, but their field-level rights, caching limits, display rules, and deletion requirements must remain explicit.

---

## 2.3 Field-level provenance is necessary

An entity may contain facts from several sources:

```text
Place identity        → open dataset
Coordinates           → open dataset
Opening hours         → operator
Event occurrence      → event feed
Price                 → operator
Photo                 → separately licensed asset
Travel time           → live routing provider
Editorial description → our own content
```

The system therefore cannot treat the entire entity as if it came from one source.

Every important claim should preserve:

- source;
- retrieval/verification time;
- rights;
- expiry/TTL;
- confidence;
- conflict state.

---

## 2.4 “Unknown” must remain a valid state

The product should not require every field to be complete.

For example:

```text
Open tonight:          confirmed
Travel time:           14 minutes
Price:                 95 SAR
Booking required:      unknown
Live slot availability: unknown
```

This may still be a useful result.

The correct product response is:

> Booking availability not confirmed — check with the venue.

The incorrect response is to infer availability or suppress every record with incomplete data.

---

## 2.5 Events and availability are harder than places

Persistent places are comparatively stable.

Events and availability are volatile.

This should affect both data architecture and product promises.

A useful distinction is:

```text
PLACES
Persistent / slow-changing

EVENTS
Short-lived / rapidly expiring

LIVE AVAILABILITY
Query-time or very short-lived
```

The project should not force all three into the same freshness or storage model.

---

# 3. The Architectural Correction

The most important correction after R01 is:

> **The product should not attempt to manually maintain a globally verified database of everything.**

That model is economically and operationally incompatible with the intended international scope.

Instead, the product should aim to become:

# A Discovery Intelligence Layer

Its long-term advantage should come from:

- query understanding;
- cross-domain discovery;
- search planning;
- geographic and temporal feasibility;
- ranking;
- diversification;
- contextual recommendations;
- planning;
- personalization;
- confidence and uncertainty handling.

The product should **consume and intelligently combine data**, not recreate every upstream data company.

---

# 4. Two Different Company Models

## Model A — “We Own the Entire Local Truth”

The system attempts to maintain:

- every place;
- every event;
- every opening hour;
- every price;
- booking rules;
- live availability;
- descriptions;
- photos;
- changes;
- closures.

This model eventually requires:

- ingestion infrastructure;
- local data operations;
- verification teams;
- vendor agreements;
- partnerships;
- moderation;
- support;
- possibly geography-specific operations.

This can become a legitimate company model at large scale, but it should **not** be the prerequisite for proving the product.

---

## Model B — “We Own the Intelligence Layer”

The system owns:

- internal identities;
- search-intent representation;
- ranking logic;
- user context;
- saved items;
- query planning;
- feasibility logic;
- source-selection logic;
- confidence handling;
- planner logic.

It obtains factual information through a mixture of:

- open data;
- licensed data;
- first-party operator feeds;
- query-time APIs;
- event feeds;
- source links;
- selective curation.

This model is substantially more compatible with international scaling.

**R01.2 adopts Model B as the primary architectural hypothesis.**

---

# 5. Revised Data Philosophy

The previous conceptual flow risked becoming:

```text
INGEST
  ↓
VERIFY EVERYTHING
  ↓
STORE
  ↓
CONTINUOUSLY REVERIFY
  ↓
SEARCH
```

R01.2 replaces it with:

```text
DISCOVER / INGEST WHAT WE MAY OWN
            ↓
BUILD A STABLE SEARCHABLE CORE
            ↓
GENERATE CANDIDATES
            ↓
VERIFY VOLATILE FACTS ONLY WHEN THEY MATTER
            ↓
RANK + EXPLAIN UNCERTAINTY
            ↓
DISPLAY RESULTS
```

The fundamental principle becomes:

# Verification should be demand-driven.

The product should spend freshness effort primarily on records that:

- are likely to be shown;
- have volatile decision-critical fields;
- are near an upcoming event;
- are frequently searched;
- have conflicting sources;
- are reported as incorrect;
- have high downstream impact.

Not every entity deserves the same refresh frequency.

---

# 6. Query-Time Verification

Consider a query:

> “Something fun indoors tonight in Khobar within 20 minutes.”

A scalable process could be:

```text
1. Search owned/open index
        ↓
150 potential candidates

2. Apply category / semantic / geo constraints
        ↓
25 candidates

3. Initial feasibility filtering
        ↓
12 candidates

4. Refresh decision-critical facts
        ↓
8 plausible candidates

5. Request route times
        ↓
5 feasible candidates

6. Rank + diversify
        ↓
3 recommended options
```

The system does **not** need to freshly verify all 150 records every week.

It verifies the small subset whose freshness actually affects the current decision.

---

# 7. Verification Should Be Hierarchical

“Verification” must not automatically mean “a human checks the record.”

The system should prefer the cheapest trustworthy method available.

## Level 1 — Trusted structured feed

Examples:

- licensed event feed;
- operator API;
- business-owned update feed;
- government open dataset.

Human involvement:

**none under normal operation.**

---

## Level 2 — Direct first-party structured data

Examples:

- operator-maintained structured endpoint;
- ICS;
- RSS;
- authorized JSON-LD or structured feed;
- business portal.

Human involvement:

**usually none after integration.**

---

## Level 3 — Automated refresh from permitted source

Where rights allow automated retrieval.

Human involvement:

**only on conflict/error.**

---

## Level 4 — Business or community update

Venue or trusted contributor supplies a correction.

Human involvement:

**moderation / anomaly handling where necessary.**

---

## Level 5 — Human editorial verification

Use only when:

- the option is strategically important;
- automated sources disagree;
- the record is unusually valuable;
- the source situation is ambiguous;
- the discovery value justifies editorial work.

Human verification should be an **exception path**, not the default freshness engine.

---

# 8. What Humans Should Actually Do

Human effort can still create differentiation.

But it should focus on work machines/providers are bad at.

Examples:

- identifying unusual local experiences;
- curating distinctive independent businesses;
- describing why something is interesting;
- resolving difficult duplicate entities;
- reviewing conflicting facts;
- evaluating ambiguous “hidden gem” claims;
- verifying sensitive accessibility claims;
- handling abuse/corrections.

Humans should **not** spend most of their time repeatedly confirming routine coordinates or ordinary opening hours if a reliable automated source exists.

A plausible long-term distribution may look conceptually like:

```text
90–95% automated / provider / operator / open data
5–10% editorial / conflict resolution / unique curation
```

These percentages are directional hypotheses, not targets.

---

# 9. Human Curation as Differentiation, Not Maintenance

A useful early test may include:

- 30–50 editorially selected discoveries;
- a much larger automatically sourced ordinary corpus.

For example:

```text
5,000 normal places / activities
    ↓
automated/open/provider-supported

40 distinctive discoveries
    ↓
carefully curated
```

This is much more scalable than:

```text
500 places
    ↓
manually rechecked every month
```

The purpose of curation should be to create **discovery quality**, not to compensate indefinitely for an unscalable data architecture.

---

# 10. International Scaling Model

The product should tolerate uneven information quality across geographies.

It should not require every location to meet the same completeness standard.

Conceptually:

```text
City A
High place coverage
High event coverage
Good routing
Good operator feeds

City B
High place coverage
Medium event coverage
Good routing
Weak availability

City C
Medium place coverage
Low event coverage
Basic routing
```

The system should know what it knows.

Its interface can adapt:

### High-confidence geography

> 8 activities open tonight.

### Medium-confidence geography

> 5 places appear open tonight. Booking status is not confirmed.

### Weak geography

> Limited verified information in this area. Showing persistent places and official sources.

International scale therefore depends partly on **confidence-aware degradation**, not identical global coverage.

---

# 11. Product Promise Levels

R01 revealed that several promises have dramatically different operational costs.

## Level 1 — Discovery

> Find relevant and interesting things around me.

Requirements:

- place/event identity;
- category;
- location;
- basic time information.

Relatively achievable.

---

## Level 2 — Feasibility

> Find options that realistically fit where I am, when I am free, and my practical constraints.

Requirements:

- hours / occurrence dates;
- travel-time estimate;
- approximate duration;
- price where relevant;
- booking requirement where known.

Harder, but still compatible with automation and uncertainty.

**This remains the strongest core product hypothesis.**

---

## Level 3 — Guaranteed live availability

> These exact options have open slots available right now and can be booked.

Requirements:

- direct inventory;
- booking systems;
- ticket integrations;
- commercial partnerships;
- near-live synchronization.

This moves the project into a substantially more operationally complex business.

**Level 3 should not be required for validating Levels 1 and 2.**

---

# 12. Revised Interpretation of “Available Tonight”

Early product language should carefully distinguish:

### Open tonight

There is credible evidence that the venue/activity operates during the requested window.

from:

### Scheduled tonight

A specific occurrence/session exists.

from:

### Available tonight

There is confirmed live capacity/bookability.

The first two may be achievable relatively early.

The third should only be claimed with inventory-level evidence.

Example UI:

> Open tonight · Booking may be required

rather than:

> Available at 9:30 PM

when live availability is unknown.

---

# 13. Revised Role of the Canonical Database

The canonical database remains valuable, but its job changes.

It should own mainly:

- internal entity IDs;
- provider crosswalks;
- open/owned facts;
- source provenance;
- category/taxonomy;
- search embeddings only for content we have rights to embed;
- editorial attributes;
- user-generated metadata;
- confidence state;
- last-known verification metadata.

It should **not** automatically attempt to own:

- every commercial provider field;
- third-party review text;
- third-party photos;
- live inventory;
- all event status;
- every volatile operational fact.

Some data may remain:

- query-time;
- cached briefly;
- source-linked;
- provider-controlled.

---

# 14. Suggested Global Architecture

```text
                 USER QUERY
                     │
                     ▼
              INTENT INTERPRETER
                     │
                     ▼
                QUERY PLANNER
                     │
         ┌───────────┼───────────┐
         │           │           │
         ▼           ▼           ▼
   OWNED / OPEN   EVENT / LIVE   FIRST-PARTY
      INDEX         SOURCES       SOURCES
         │           │           │
         └───────────┼───────────┘
                     │
                     ▼
              CANDIDATE FUSION
                     │
                     ▼
             FEASIBILITY GATE
                     │
      ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
      │              │              │
      ▼              ▼              ▼
 TIME REFRESH   ROUTE CHECK   FACT CONFIDENCE
      │              │              │
      └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                     │
                     ▼
             RANK + DIVERSIFY
                     │
                     ▼
          RESULT + SOURCE + CAVEAT
```

This architecture allows the system to combine:

- persistent owned knowledge;
- live restricted information;
- direct first-party facts;
- query-time calculations;

without forcing all upstream content into one permanently stored corpus.

---

# 15. Provider Strategy

The target should not be:

> Find one global provider that gives us everything.

That would create extreme dependency.

The system should instead have replaceable provider roles.

For example:

```text
Place Identity Provider
Event Provider
Routing Provider
Geocoder
First-Party Feed
Media Provider
```

Each role should have:

- primary source;
- fallback source;
- source-specific rights;
- clear failure behavior.

The intelligence layer remains ours even if a supplier changes.

---

# 16. Business-Maintained Data

If the platform gains traction, businesses and venues should eventually be able to claim their entity and maintain volatile fields.

Examples:

```text
Verified Venue

Hours
Price
Activities
Upcoming sessions
Booking URL
Age restrictions
Images
```

This converts some freshness cost from:

```text
Our team → manually checking venues
```

to:

```text
Venue → maintaining its own operational facts
Our system → validating / moderating
```

The product should eventually provide incentives for operators to keep information current.

This is not required for the MVP, but it is an important scalability path.

---

# 17. Event Architecture Should Differ From Place Architecture

The system should not treat events like ordinary place rows.

## Place index

Characteristics:

- long-lived;
- identity-focused;
- slower refresh;
- persistent.

## Event / occurrence index

Characteristics:

- expires;
- date-bounded;
- cancellation-sensitive;
- often source-owned;
- may be fetched frequently;
- may be deleted automatically after completion.

## Live inventory

Characteristics:

- extremely volatile;
- should usually remain at source;
- query-time;
- potentially unavailable.

This separation should influence R03's eventual conceptual model.

---

# 18. Revised Cost Philosophy

The important question is no longer:

> What does it cost to manually verify 500 records each month?

The better question is:

> **How much useful discovery coverage can we achieve with automation before human work becomes necessary?**

Costs should be measured in layers.

## Layer A — Persistent baseline

- open datasets;
- storage;
- indexing;
- taxonomy.

Expected recurring marginal cost:

relatively low.

---

## Layer B — Query-time intelligence

- routing;
- live provider lookups;
- semantic search;
- intent parsing.

Cost grows mainly with actual usage.

This is desirable because:

> cost scales with users.

---

## Layer C — Exceptional human review

- high-value curation;
- conflict resolution;
- corrections;
- unusual discoveries.

Cost should scale with the subset of data where human judgment creates value.

---

## Layer D — Optional future commercial integrations

- ticket inventory;
- bookings;
- premium feeds.

Only justified once demand proves their value.

---

# 19. New Economic Principle

A core economic design goal should be:

> **Avoid large fixed data-maintenance cost before user demand exists.**

Prefer:

```text
low fixed cost
+
usage-based variable cost
```

over:

```text
large recurring manual verification team
+
unknown user demand
```

A first useful international product should ideally be operable by:

- a small development team;
- open/licensed data;
- cloud infrastructure;
- selective APIs;
- automation;
- limited editorial effort.

If a single Saudi test geography requires a standing manual verification operation before meaningful usage exists, the architecture should be reconsidered.

---

# 20. Roadmap Correction

R01 originally leads naturally into a traditional coverage audit.

R01.2 changes the objective of the next research step.

## Previous implied R02 question

> How many records can we verify and maintain in a candidate area?

## Revised R02 question

> **How much useful, decision-ready discovery coverage can we generate automatically or on demand, with minimal recurring human maintenance?**

This is a major roadmap correction.

# 21. Revised R02 Objectives

R02 should test:

## 21.1 Automatic place coverage

Using open / rights-clear sources such as:

- FSQ OS;
- Overture;
- optionally separately governed OSM data;
- Wikidata;
- licensed Saudi open datasets.

Measure:

- entity coverage;
- branch accuracy;
- category quality;
- geographic accuracy;
- duplication.

---

## 21.2 Automatic field completeness

For the test content families, measure how often we automatically obtain:

- hours;
- official URL;
- price;
- duration;
- booking rule;
- occurrence date;
- age rule;
- category.

Do not manually fill every missing field during the first measurement.

The missing-data rate itself is an important result.

---

## 21.3 Query-time refresh potential

For candidates that would actually be surfaced, measure:

- which facts can be refreshed automatically;
- from which permitted sources;
- latency;
- API cost;
- success rate;
- freshness improvement.

---

## 21.4 Travel-time automation

Test route calls only after geographic pruning.

Measure:

- cost per search;
- accuracy;
- provider differences;
- whether rough geo filtering reduces calls sufficiently.

---

## 21.5 Human exception rate

After automation, measure:

> What percentage of useful candidates genuinely require human intervention?

This is more important than total verification hours.

Examples:

```text
1,000 candidate records

850 usable automatically
100 usable with query-time refresh
35 require operator confirmation
15 require human conflict review
```

This distribution would support scalability.

A distribution where 60% require manual attention would be a warning sign.

---

## 21.6 Value of curated local discoveries

Separately test a small manually curated set.

Measure whether these records materially improve:

- novelty;
- user interest;
- result diversity;
- discovery beyond obvious locations.

This lets us determine whether editorial effort is:

> high-value differentiation

rather than:

> basic database maintenance.

---

# 22. Revised Success Metric for R02

The main R02 metric should become something like:

# Automation Coverage Ratio

Conceptually:

```text
decision-ready candidates produced
without human intervention
----------------------------------
all decision-ready candidates
```

Additional metrics:

- % of place identities automated;
- % of critical fields automatically available;
- % refreshed query-time;
- % requiring manual intervention;
- cost per automated candidate;
- cost per search;
- percentage of results containing uncertainty;
- error rate;
- missing-field rate;
- time to produce final top-3 results.

Exact formulas should be refined during R02.

---

# 23. Architecture Decision Gate After R02

R02 should answer:

### Gate A

Can open/licensed sources create enough baseline discovery density?

### Gate B

Can volatile facts be refreshed automatically for the shortlist?

### Gate C

Can the system produce useful top results while allowing some fields to remain unknown?

### Gate D

Is the human exception rate low enough to support international scaling?

### Gate E

Does small-scale human curation create enough additional discovery quality to justify itself?

If Gates A–D fail, the project should reconsider:

- the product promise;
- the content scope;
- source strategy;
- geography;
- or business model.

It should **not** immediately respond by hiring more people to verify data.

# 25. Architecture Principles Added by R01.2

The project should carry these forward as explicit principles.

## Principle 1

**Do not build a global manual verification operation before product-market evidence.**

## Principle 2

**Verification effort should follow demand and volatility.**

## Principle 3

**Unknown is preferable to fabricated certainty.**

## Principle 4

**The system should degrade gracefully in weak-data geographies.**

## Principle 5

**Humans should improve differentiation and resolve exceptions, not maintain ordinary facts at global scale.**

## Principle 6

**Place, event, and live-availability data need different freshness models.**

## Principle 7

**Restricted provider content may remain query-time or provider-controlled instead of being forced into the owned database.**

## Principle 8

**The intelligence layer — intent, retrieval, feasibility, ranking, planning — is the primary product asset.**

## Principle 9

**Prefer variable cost tied to real usage over large fixed data-maintenance cost.**

## Principle 10

**A missing field should trigger uncertainty handling, not automatically human verification.**

# 26. What R01.2 Explicitly Rejects

R01.2 rejects the following as default assumptions:

- every place must be manually verified;
- every field must be complete;
- every event must exist in our permanent database;
- availability must be guaranteed;
- every city requires local verification staff;
- all records need the same refresh cadence;
- the product must own every factual field it displays;
- a result with an unknown field is unusable;
- international expansion requires manually recreating the database in each market.

These may occasionally be appropriate for a specific premium feature, but they are not the default architecture.

---

# 27. What R01.2 Does Not Reject

This document does **not** mean:

- freshness does not matter;
- inaccurate data is acceptable;
- humans are unnecessary;
- provider terms can be ignored;
- scraping becomes acceptable;
- every field can be fetched live;
- APIs are automatically cheap;
- open data is automatically accurate;
- query-time calls solve all rights issues.

The goal is not lower quality.

The goal is:

> **achieve trustworthy usefulness through selective, scalable verification rather than blanket manual maintenance.**

---

# 28. Revised Product Thesis

The product should increasingly be understood as:

> **A global discovery intelligence system that combines persistent open/owned knowledge with live and first-party information to find options that fit a user's time, location, and intent — while explicitly representing uncertainty when facts cannot be confirmed.**

The long-term product asset is therefore not simply:

> a giant places database.

It is:

```text
INTENT
  ×
DATA ORCHESTRATION
  ×
TIME
  ×
GEOGRAPHY
  ×
FEASIBILITY
  ×
RANKING
  ×
USER CONTEXT
```

# 29. R01 + R01.2 Combined Decision

R01 remains the source-rights foundation.

R01.2 modifies the architecture and economic interpretation.

Together they imply:

### Keep

- open/owned place identities;
- rights-segregated data;
- source provenance;
- cautious use of restricted APIs;
- first-party operator data;
- event-specific handling;
- query-time route calculation;
- uncertainty.

### Reduce

- blanket manual verification;
- assumptions of complete fields;
- persistent storage of every volatile fact;
- pre-launch operations burden.

### Increase

- automation;
- demand-triggered refresh;
- live/query-time enrichment;
- provider abstraction;
- confidence-aware results;
- operator-maintained data;
- selective curation.

# 30. Required Change to R02

The next research artifact should therefore be:

# R02 — Automated Coverage & Feasibility Audit

Its first question should be:

> **How far can we get toward the first discovery promise using open/licensed baseline data, automated/query-time refresh, and source confidence — before a human needs to intervene?**

Its second question should be:

> **When human intervention is needed, is it exceptional and high-value, or is it systemic?**

That distinction will tell us whether the architecture can plausibly support international scale.

# 31. Decision

**R01 remains accepted as a source-rights and economics study.**

**The manual-verification scenario is retained only as a warning/stress case, not as the target operating model.**

The project now adopts an **automation-first, query-time-enrichment, confidence-aware discovery architecture** as the leading hypothesis going into R02.

The next study should try to falsify that hypothesis.

If R02 demonstrates that most meaningful results still require recurring human verification, the project should reconsider the product promise or data strategy before increasing operational headcount.

---

# 32. Core Takeaway

The project is trying to build:

> **an international discovery product**

not:

> **a manually maintained global local-information company.**

A global information company may eventually emerge if demand justifies it.

It should not be required in order to prove the product.

The architecture must therefore make **automation the default, live verification selective, uncertainty explicit, and human effort strategically scarce.**
